Part 1 · Blood flukes

Schistosoma

Schistosomes are the causative agents of the disease schistosomiasis. Three species of schistosomes that parasitize humans are of major importance: S. mansoni, S. haematobium and S. japonicum.

The two intestinal species compared

DiseaseIntestinal bilharziasisIntestinal bilharziasis
DistributionNile Delta, Africa, South America, Middle EastFar East
Intermediate hostBiomphalaria alexandrinaOncomelania species
Reservoir hostMonkeys, rodentsDogs, cats, rodents, pigs and cattle
HabitatInferior mesenteric plexus of veinsSuperior mesenteric plexus of veins
Editorial note

The source introduces the table as covering “the three species”, but it gives only S. mansoni and S. japonicum — the two intestinal species. No S. haematobium column is supplied, so none is added here.

Life cycle

Eggs escape to the perivascular tissue due to
  1. Pressure within the venule.
  2. Effect of the spine.
  3. The oozing lytic secretion of the miracidium within the egg.

From the perivascular tissue, eggs pass to the lumen of the intestine (S. mansoni, S. japonicum) or of the urinary bladder (S. haematobium).

Cercariae penetrate the skin, aided by
  1. Proteolytic enzymes secreted from penetration glands.
  2. The surface tension of drying droplets of water.
  3. The strong lashing movements of the tail pressing the body of the cercaria into the skin.

When the embryonated eggs (diagnostic stage) discharged in faeces enter fresh water, miracidia hatch from the eggs and rapidly swim by means of cilia (they survive for 24 hours). They then penetrate the body of the snail (intermediate host).

Drinking water may lead to infection when cercariae penetrate the mucosa above the level of gastric acidity, which kills them. Cercariae lose their tails and transform into schistosomula.

Migration inside the definitive host — in the source’s order
Venous circulationschistosomula migrate to the lungs
Through the lungsbreak out through the pulmonary capillaries; carried through the left heart into the systemic circulation
Liverreach the intra-hepatic branches of the portal veins, settle down and mature
Pairing → egg layingwithin several weeks, paired worms migrate to the mesenteric veins (S. mansoni, S. japonicum) or vesical plexus (S. haematobium)

Prepatent period (cercarial penetration → eggs in the excreta): about 35 days for S. mansoni and S. japonicum, and about 70 days for S. haematobium.

Pathogenesis and clinical picture

Intestinal schistosomiasis: 7–8 weeks for S. mansoni infection and 5–6 weeks for S. japonicum infection.

Editorial note

The source sentence reads “Intestinal schistosomiasis is 7-8 weeks … and 5-6 weeks” without naming which period is meant. It is kept as printed rather than guessed.

Pathological changes are caused mainly by schistosome eggs deposited in various tissues. The antigens excreted from embryonated eggs result in formation of granulomas that lead eventually to fibrotic changes in the tissues involved in chronic infections. Thus, the lesions produced are essentially the same among the 3 major species. There are 4 progressive stages:

1 · Invasion1–4 days

Skin lesion due to cercarial penetration: local dermatitis, irritation, papular rash.

2 · Migration3–4 weeks
  • Lung: verminous pneumonitis, minute haemorrhages, cough and haemoptysis.
  • Liver: enlarged and tender.

Metabolic products of migrating parasites → toxic and allergic manifestations: urticaria, eosinophilia, leukocytosis, fever, headache and muscle pain.

3 · Egg deposition & extrusionacute stage · 1–2 months

Eggs deposited in the venous plexus escape from the veins into the perivascular tissue and finally find their way to the outside with urine and stool (open-lesion stage). With extrusion of eggs through the wall of the intestine or bladder there may again be generalized malaise, fever, rigors, urticaria, abdominal pain and liver tenderness.

Intestinal bilharziasis (S. mansoni, S. japonicum): active egg deposition → tissue damage and haemorrhage. Symptoms: dysentery with blood and mucus in stool, abdominal pain. S. japonicum may present with Katayama fever.

4 · Tissue reactionchronic stage

Eggs trapped in the tissue secrete soluble egg antigen that stimulates inflammatory reactions (eosinophilic granulomas) and fibrosis → bilharzial nodules, papillomata, sandy patches and reduced egg output (closed-lesion stage).

Intestine (S. mansoni, S. japonicum): the wall becomes fibrosed and thickened and may be complicated with strictures, sinuses, fistulae and prolapse.

Hepatosplenic disease — the serious end of the chronic stage

Eggs that fail to be fixed in the venules of the intestinal wall fall into the lumen and are swept by the blood to the liver → periportal fibrosis, portal hypertension, hepatosplenomegaly, followed by a shrunken fibrosed liver.

Portal hypertension → oesophageal varices, haemorrhoids and ascites, manifested by haematemesis, melena and a distended abdomen. This stage ends in hepatocellular failure (impaired liver function) and fatal haemorrhage. The enlarged spleen and hypersplenism destroy red blood cells, adding to the existing anaemia; later this leads to pancytopenia.

Diagnosis of intestinal schistosomiasis

ApproachDetails
1 · ClinicalLiving in or visiting endemic areas with a history of exposure to canal water, and complaining of dysentery or hepatosplenomegaly, is suggestive.
2 · ParasitologicalStool examination for recovery of the eggs (diagnostic stage) by (a) direct smear; (b) concentration techniques, which may be required when infection is light.
3 · ImmunodiagnosisDetection of antigens and antibodies, as in urinary schistosomiasis.
4 · Other tests(a) CBC: eosinophilia up to 40% and anaemia. (b) Rectal swab. (c) Colonoscopy and sigmoidoscopy for visualizing lesions (e.g. papillomata) and for biopsies. (d) Ultrasonography and other imaging: periportal fibrosis and hepatosplenomegaly.
Editorial note

“As in urinary schistosomiasis” refers to material not included in this chapter. In item (c) the source also lists a lesion printed illegibly (“cologic peiyrosis”); it is omitted rather than guessed.

Treatment (as given in the source)

Praziquantel (Biltricide), oxamniquine (Vansil) and metrifonate.

Prevention and control
  1. Mass treatment and follow-up of infected persons.
  2. Health education.
  3. Snail control.
Clinical clarification · treatment

Praziquantel remains the drug of choice for schistosomiasis and is effective against all major Schistosoma species (CDC). Oxamniquine acts only on S. mansoni and is no longer readily available; metrifonate is active only against S. haematobium, so it does not treat intestinal disease.

Sources: CDC, Clinical Care of Schistosomiasis; Colley DG, “Human schistosomiasis” (CDC Stacks).

Part 2 · Tapeworms

Taenia saginata — beef tapeworm

Also called the bald tapeworm. Cosmopolitan, especially in cattle-raising countries.

HabitatSmall intestine
Definitive hostMan (the only definitive host)
Intermediate hostCattle
Infective stageCysticercus bovis in cattle meat
Diagnostic stageMature egg in stool
Mode of infection

Ingestion of undercooked beef containing viable cysticerci, usually as hamburgers or grilled meat (kebab). In the small intestine the scolex is evaginated, attaches to the mucosa and matures in about 3 months. The adult lives in the small intestine, attached to the mucosa.

Pathogenesis and clinical picture

  1. Intestinal disturbances, hunger pains and diarrhoea.
  2. Neurologic manifestations.
  3. Intestinal obstruction.
  4. Loss of weight — the parasite consumes much of the patient’s food, leading to malnutrition and avitaminosis.
  5. Appendicitis or cholangitis caused by stray segments of the worm.
  6. Migrating segments creeping out of the anus cause irritation, itching and worry.

Diagnosis

1 · ClinicalSuggestive symptoms such as diarrhoea and weight loss, with passage of gravid segments through the anus.
2 · ParasitologicalStool examination for eggs and proglottids (diagnostic stages). Recovery of eggs from the perianal region using a swab. Gravid segments in faeces — if not found, give a saline purge. Compress the segments between two slides and count the main lateral branches.
3 · ImmunodiagnosisDetection of coproantigen in stool by ELISA.
Treatment
  1. Praziquantel is the drug of choice for both T. saginata and T. solium; it causes worm paralysis — 10 mg/kg as a single oral dose.
  2. Yomesan (niclosamide): a single oral dose of 2 g on an empty stomach.
Control
  1. Treatment of infected persons.
  2. Avoid grazing cattle on fields fertilized by human manure.
  3. Proper meat inspection in slaughterhouses.
  4. Refrigeration of meat at −10 °C for 5 days.
  5. Thorough cooking of beef.
Clinical clarification · taeniasis treatment

CDC gives praziquantel 5–10 mg/kg orally once (10 mg/kg may cure more often) and niclosamide 2 g once as an alternative; WHO lists the same single-dose options. CDC advises using praziquantel and albendazole carefully in anyone suspected of having cysticercosis (seizures have been reported) — relevant to T. solium below.

Sources: CDC, Clinical Care of Taeniasis; WHO, Taeniasis/cysticercosis fact sheet. The −10 °C / 5-day freezing figure was not independently re-verified.

2.2Taenia solium — pork tapeworm

Cosmopolitan where pork is eaten; thus it is rare in Islamic and Jewish communities. Similar to T. saginata, but differs in:

FeatureT. soliumT. saginata (for contrast, from above)
Intermediate hostPigCattle
Larval stage (infective)Cysticercus cellulosae — invaginated scolex with four suckers and hooksCysticercus bovis
Infection byIngestion of undercooked porkUndercooked beef

Pathogenesis and clinical picture

  1. Intestinal disturbances, neurologic manifestations, intestinal obstruction or loss of weight.
  2. When man ingests eggs of T. solium, the larval stage (cysticercus cellulosae) develops in extra-intestinal tissues → cysticercosis. This occurs with T. solium, not with T. saginata — which makes T. solium run a more serious course.
Diagnosis
  1. Clinical diagnosis.
  2. Laboratory: stool examination for the gravid segments. Eggs are rarely present in stool and are similar to those of T. saginata.
Treatment (as given in the source)
  1. Similar to that of T. saginata.
  2. Yomesan 2 g single oral dose on an empty stomach, then a saline purge 1–2 hours later to avoid cysticercosis from eggs of disintegrated segments. Quinacrine hydrochloride (atebrine) can be added, as it expels the parasite intact.
  3. Give an antiemetic before treatment to avoid vomiting.

Part 3 · Intestinal nematodes

Ascaris lumbricoides — giant intestinal roundworm

DistributionCosmopolitan; more prevalent in warm countries with bad sanitation. Common in Egypt (especially children).
HostMan
HabitatSmall intestine (free in the lumen)
Life cycleGeohelminthic infection
Infection

Infection of man takes place by swallowing water or raw vegetables polluted with embryonated eggs containing the infective larva, or through hands contaminated by polluted soil.

Pathogenesis and clinical picture

Tissue damage may be due to: (1) the large size of Ascaris (largest intestinal nematode); (2) adults do not attach to the intestinal wall, so they may go to ectopic places; (3) toxic products stimulate an immune response.

The usual infection (10–20 worms) often passes unnoticed. In heavier infection:

1 · During larval migration

Intense local inflammatory reaction around the larvae → pneumonitis: fever, cough, dyspnoea and eosinophilia.

In the intestine

Abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, colic, distension or dyspepsia, due to production of anti-enzymes that interfere with protein digestion → malnutrition and underdevelopment of children. Changes of bowel habit (diarrhoea or constipation).

2 · Complications

Traumatic effects — adult worms irritated by fever, drugs or anaesthesia. A mass of worms causes intestinal obstruction, volvulus and intussusception. Worms are not fixed, so they may wander to:

Bile ducts → obstructive jaundiceLiver (carrying intestinal bacteria) → liver abscesses
Appendix → appendicitisAmpulla of Vater → acute haemorrhagic pancreatitis
Peritoneum → peritonitisStomach → vomiting, or escape through the external nares
Trachea (very rarely) → suffocation

Toxic effects — by-products of living and dead worms in sensitized persons: oedema of the face, urticaria, asthma, insomnia, nervous irritability and convulsions.

Diagnosis, treatment and prevention

Diagnosis

1 · Clinical: non-specific.
2 · Parasitological: eggs in stool; larvae in sputum and Charcot–Leyden crystals early in the disease.
3 · Other: high eosinophilia; X-ray after barium meal shows worms as filling defects.

Treatment

Mebendazole or flubendazole: 100 mg twice daily for 3 days.

Prevention and control

1 · Purification and chlorination of water. 2 · Washing raw vegetables; washing hands before meals. 3 · Proper disposal of sewage (human faeces). 4 · Mass treatment, personal hygiene and education.

3.2Enterobius vermicularis — pinworm, seatworm

DistributionCosmopolitan; more in temperate and cold climates
Final hostMan, especially children
HabitatLarge intestine: mainly caecum, colon, rectum and appendix
Mode of infection
  1. Ingestion of infective eggs through autoinfection (contaminated hand to mouth) or contaminated food or drinks.
  2. Airborne infection through inhalation of eggs in dust particles.

Pathogenesis and symptoms

  1. Irritation, itching and scratching of the perianal skin (pruritus ani) → insomnia, nervous manifestations and secondary bacterial infection.
  2. Worms in young girls may deposit eggs on the vulva → pruritus vulvae.
  3. Worms may migrate to the vagina (vaginitis), uterus and urinary bladder (urethritis), causing irritation and inflammation.
  4. Appendicitis due to migrating adults.
  5. Because infection spreads easily within a family, all the family should be examined and all infected members treated at the same time.
  6. Sometimes the vagina and bladder are invaded in female patients, causing irritation.
  7. Migration to the peritoneal cavity (through the uterine tubes) causes pelvic peritonitis.
Diagnosis

Clinically: suspected in children with pruritus ani at night.
Laboratory: adult worms may be seen in stool or the anal area. Eggs are rarely found in stool (about 5% only). Swab the anal / perianal area with (a) an NIH swab (National Institutes of Health) or (b) a Scotch adhesive-tape swab.

Treatment (as given in the source)

1 · Albendazole, mebendazole.
2 · Local application of white oxide of mercury (white precipitate ointment) around the anus at night (relieves itching, kills female worms coming out, prevents dispersal of eggs).

Prevention and control

Personal hygiene. Infected persons should sleep alone; their underwear and bedding handled and washed carefully. Food and drink protected from dust and hands.

Clinical clarification · pinworm treatment

CDC’s regimen is a single dose repeated after 2 weeks — mebendazole 100 mg, albendazole 400 mg, or pyrantel pamoate 11 mg/kg (max 1 g) — because the drugs kill worms but not eggs. Household members are treated together when more than one is infected or infection recurs. The mercury ointment in item 2 is not part of current CDC treatment guidance.

Sources: CDC, Clinical Overview of Pinworm Infection; CDC Yellow Book 2024, Enterobiasis.

Part 4 · Extra-intestinal amoebiasis

Hepatic amoebiasis

Pathogenesis

a · Direct spread

Of amoebic trophozoites from a primary intestinal lesion to surrounding tissues.

b · Haematogenous spread

Through invasion of blood: the portal circulation draining the intestine carries amoebic emboli mainly to the right lobe of the liver.

Liver affection may take the form of diffuse amoebic hepatitis or liver abscess.

Diffuse amoebic hepatitisIn a considerable number of patients with amoebic colitis there is an associated enlargement of the liver. It starts with constant invasion of the liver by amoebae from an active intestinal process via the portal circulation.
Amoebic liver abscessWhere amoebic colonization continues there is lytic necrosis of hepatic parenchyma. Lesions coalesce, typically forming a solitary lesion that begins as a small area of necrosis and enlarges, with liquefaction in the centre and thickening of the wall. Contents: lysed liver cells, bile, fat, RBCs and collagen strands — a viscid chocolate-red or creamy mass (“anchovy-sauce” pus). Trophozoites are found only in the edge of the lesion, where they multiply. Commonest site: the posterior part of the right lobe (silent area), causing upward bulging of the diaphragm. The abscess may become chronic, absorbed, fibrosed, calcified, or extend to other organs.

Clinical picture

Symptoms

Usually an acute onset of upper-abdominal pain with high fever. The pain is intense and constant, radiating to the scapular region and right shoulder, increasing with deep breathing or coughing. Fever 38–40 °C; anorexia, nausea, vomiting and fatigue.

Signs
  1. Enlarged tender liver
  2. Pallor
  3. Earthy look
  4. Fever
  5. Tinge of jaundice

Differential diagnosis: other causes of hepatomegaly and cystic lesions in the liver (hydatid disease).

Diagnosis

a · ClinicalThe clinical picture.
b · LaboratoryIndirect (serology) for specific antibodies: IHAT (indirect haemagglutination) and ELISA are sensitive and specific but cannot assess cure, because antibodies persist for a long time.
Direct: (i) aspiration of the abscess — specimen from the border for trophozoites; (ii) stool examination — positive in only one third of cases; (iii) copro-antigen detection by enzyme immunoassay.
c · RadiologyX-ray: high density in the hepatic shadow and abnormal elevation of the right dome of the diaphragm. Ultrasonography, computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance may visualize the abscess.
d · CBCPolymorphonuclear leucocytosis in amoebic liver abscess (differentiates it from viral hepatitis).
Prevention and control
  1. Personal hygiene.
  2. Washing fresh-eaten vegetables and fruits.
  3. Safe water supply; water from a polluted source should be boiled.
  4. Insect control, or protection of food from insects.
  5. Treatment of patients and examination of contacts.

The source chapter gives no treatment for hepatic amoebiasis; none is added here.
