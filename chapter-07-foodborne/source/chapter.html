Foodborne illness

Salmonella: poultry are considered the primary reservoirs of the organism causing:

Salmonellosis or enteric fever (typhoid)

Resulting from bacterial invasion of the bloodstream.

Acute gastroenteritis

Resulting from a food-borne infection mainly contaminated eggs, and intoxication.

Editorial note

The handout’s outline line reads “a. Enteric fever · b. Helicobacter pylori–Campylobacter pylori”. The handout text contains no separate H. pylori section; Campylobacter is covered in section 1.4 and enteric fever in 1.2.

From the earlier edition

Foodborne illness is described as infections caused by a variety of microorganisms, mainly Salmonella, Clostridium perfringens, Campylobacter and Staphylococcus aureus; Norovirus is also listed.

Salmonella is a member of the Enterobacteriaceae family.

Salmonella — the organism

Salmonella · microbiology at a glance
MorphologyGram-negative bacilli, motile, non-spore forming.
CultureFacultative anaerobes; grow well at 37 °C after 24 hours on MacConkey agar: non-lactose fermenters (pale yellow colonies). Also SS agar, deoxycholate citrate agar (DCA) and enrichment broth media, which include selenite and tetrathionate.
Biochemical reactionsPartial sugar fermentation, with production of acid and gas by most species except S. typhi, which produces acid only. Urease negative, oxidase negative, H2S positive.
Antigenic structureO (somatic) antigens and H antigen (flagella) antigens: used to determine the type within the groups. Virulent antigen (Vi Ag): anti-phagocytic action, present only in S. typhi.

Earlier edition: O antigens subdivide the Salmonellae into groups; H antigens determine the type within the groups.

PathogenesisDue to endotoxins and exotoxins (e.g. enterotoxins causing diarrhea and body-fluid loss) and antigens.
ResistanceKilled by heating at 60 °C for 30 min; antibiotics and other chemotherapy agents are effective. Resistant to the action of bile salts.

AEnteric fever

Caused by S. typhi; the milder paratyphoid fever is caused by Paratyphi A, B and C. Enteric fever is defined as a generalized infection of the reticuloendothelial system and lymphoid tissue accompanied by sustained fever and bacteremia. Enteric fevers are endemic in Egypt.

Pathogenesis and clinical picture

Enteric fever is a strictly human disease, from case or carrier stool and urine. The organisms enter the human digestive tract with contaminated food or drink, e.g. raw shellfish, milk products and raw vegetables.

From the earlier edition

Salmonella organisms survive intracellularly and replicate in macrophages due to decreased fusion of lysosomes with phagosomes, as well as the presence of surface proteins (defensins) which allow the bacteria to withstand oxygen-dependent and oxygen-independent intracellular killing. When released from macrophages, the Vi capsular antigen (only in S. typhi) withstands complement-mediated killing.

Earlier-edition wording kept as printed (“surface proteins defensins”).

Clinical course

Incubation

10–14 days

Fever

Gradually elevated in the first week, then steady in the second week.

Organs and blood

Enlargement of spleen and liver. White cell count normal or low.

After cure

Salmonella excretion by patients may continue long after clinical cure.

Outcome: 5% of patients cured from typhoid remain carriers for months or even years. Urinary carriers are common in Egypt because of bilharziasis.

Laboratory diagnosis

Specimens are collected according to the stages of the disease.

First week

Blood culture is done in fluid media, then subcultured on solid media, mainly MacConkey’s medium at 37 °C.

Second week

Salmonella is isolated from stool by direct culture on broth and solid differential selective media, then from urine by direct culture on solid media.

Serology · Widal

Antibodies rise sharply during the second and third weeks of enteric fevers.

From the earlier edition

Isolation is listed as: (A) first week — blood culture; (B) second week — stool, “and after the second week from urine”, by direct culture on selective media. The updated handout places urine culture in the second week, after stool.

Serological diagnosis by the Widal agglutination test

This test demonstrates agglutination of the patient’s serum antibodies against the O (somatic) antigens of Salmonella and the H (flagellar) antigens of S. typhi and Paratyphi A, B, C. Demonstration of rising titers in paired samples indicates acute recent infection. The results should include the titer of both O and H antibodies and Vi antibodies. The presence of IgM is indicative of acute recent infection.

1
O antigenHigh or rising titer of antibody to O antigens (≥ 1:160) suggests that active infection is present.
2
H antigenHigh titer of antibody to one type of H antigen (≥ 1:160) suggests recent infection with this type of Salmonella.
3
Vi antigenHigh titer of antibody to the Vi antigen occurs in some carriers.
4
O + several HHigh titers of antibodies to O antigens and more than one H antigen indicate recent immunization. With old immunization or past infection, O antibodies disappear and only antibodies to H antigens remain.
Highlighted in the handout

The Widal test has many limitations as a diagnostic serological test for enteric fever. Several factors need to be considered for interpretation:

  1. Endemicity of enteric fever in the area of investigation. Healthy persons have antibody titer due to subclinical infection. Titers up to 1:160 are considered normal in Egypt. False-positive results may occur in an endemic area, with immunization with any typhoid vaccine, with a previous infection, or with infection by other related members of Enterobacteriaceae.
  2. False negative in case of administration of antibiotics, and if the Widal test is done in the early stage of disease, where low antibody titers are found.

Antibiotics may not be effective on Salmonella carriage because the site of carriage may not allow penetration by the antibiotic, e.g. due to the presence of fibrous tissue.

These lines appear twice on the handout’s pages 2–3; they are given once here.

Immunity

Persistent immunity is acquired after enteric disease. CMI (cell-mediated immunity) is the main immunity. Humoral immunity destroys the organism in the blood. Antibody titer can continue for a long time after recovery.

Prevention and control — immunization

Four types of typhoid vaccines are currently available for use:

bAcetone-inactivated parenteral vaccine—
cOral live-attenuated vaccineFor S. typhi. Should not be given to children younger than 6 years of age.
dVi capsular polysaccharide (ViCPS)A newly licensed capsular polysaccharide vaccine for parenteral (intramuscular) use.

No typhoid vaccine is 100% effective and is not a substitute for good hygiene.

Clinical clarification

The vaccine statements above (“current vaccine used in Egypt”, ViCPS “newly licensed”) are the handout’s and are kept for study. The current WHO typhoid fact sheet describes typhoid conjugate vaccines (two prequalified since December 2017) and says the older vaccines do not give long-lasting immunity. Always follow current national guidance in practice.

Source: WHO, Typhoid fact sheet (who.int).

BSalmonella food poisoning

Salmonella typhimurium — food-borne toxic infection

About 12–24 hours after ingestion of contaminated food with a large number of organisms, symptoms appear (diarrhea, vomiting, fever) and last 2–5 days. Spontaneous cure usually occurs. Contamination of meat with rat excreta most often occurs.

Salmonella enteritidis — egg-associated salmonellosis

The egg can be inside perfectly normal. Incubation period 12 to 72 hours after consuming a contaminated food. The illness usually lasts 4 to 7 days, and most persons recover without antibiotic treatment.

From the earlier edition

For S. typhimurium the earlier chapter words it as: symptoms appear after 12–24 hours following ingestion of meat contaminated with rat excreta which contain a large number of organisms; it lasts 2–5 days.

Campylobacter

12–15 species are associated with human disease:

SpeciesSource named in the handout
Campylobacter jejunipoultry; humans
Campylobacter coliporcine; humans
Campylobacter fetuscattle; sheep
Microbiology
MorphologyGram-negative curved bacilli (S-shaped or “gull-winged” appearance), non-spore forming, with a characteristic rapid darting motility by means of a long sheathed polar flagellum at one (polar) or both (bipolar) ends. Motility slows quickly in wet-mount microscopy preparations due to aerotoxicity (oxygen sensitivity).
CultureMicro-aerophilic, thermophilic and capnophilic (CO2 10%), on selective media with added antibiotics such as Skirrow’s medium at 42 °C, which is the natural body temperature of avian species.
Biochemical reactionsOxidase positive, catalase positive, do not utilize carbohydrates.
Virulence factorsCellular components: endotoxin (LPS), flagellum, adhesins and invasins.
Extracellular components: enterotoxins (LT) and cytotoxins.

Transmission and clinical picture

Transmission: feco-oral route. Campylobacter is usually acquired from food (specially poultry), milk, or contact with infected animals (e.g. dogs). Children under 5 years and young adults are the most frequently affected age groups.

Incubation period of 1–7 days with acute onset, abdominal cramps, nausea but not vomiting. The bacteria damage the mucosal surfaces of the jejunum, ileum and colon. Stools may contain mucus or gross blood. Generally self-limiting; symptoms may last for a week or longer. Bacteremia is common and Campylobacter is isolated from blood.

Important late-onset complications following gastroenteritis

Guillain-Barré syndrome · Reiter’s syndrome · reactive arthritis · spontaneous abortion

Laboratory diagnosis

Specimen collection and processing: feces should be examined within a few hours. Care is needed to avoid oxygen exposure while taking the sample — or, better, a special transport medium is used.

  • Direct examination: Gram-stained smears of stools show S-shaped bacilli, and darting motility in wet-mount preparation under dark-field illumination.
  • Culture (see Culture above).  Earlier edition: culture is on Skirrow’s medium.

Antibiotic resistance: fluoroquinolone resistance has developed rapidly, related to unrestricted use and the use of enrofloxacin in poultry. The organism is inherently resistant to trimethoprim and most cephalosporins.

Brucella and brucellosis

Brucella is the cause of brucellosis, a zoonotic disease. It is a virulent, highly infectious bacterium and a facultative intracellular pathogen surviving in macrophages.

Species named in the handout
SpeciesHost
Brucella abortuscattle
Brucella melitensisgoats, sheep
Brucella suisswine, pigs
Brucella canisdogs

Species are differentiated by production of urease and H2S, dye sensitivity, cell-wall antigens and phage sensitivity.

Microbiology
MorphologyGram-negative small bacilli, non-spore forming, non-motile and non-capsulated.
CultureAerobic, 10% CO2; require enriched media containing serum, e.g. serum dextrose agar. Colonies appear after 48 to 72 hours.
Biochemical reactionsPositive for catalase, oxidase, urease, and reduce nitrates.

Brucellosis (Malta fever or undulant fever): a true zoonotic disease, as human-to-human transmission has not been identified. The disease is characterized by acute undulating fever, i.e. bouts of fever for 3–4 weeks, then 3–4 weeks without fever.

Editorial note

“B. melintensis” in the handout is spelled here as B. melitensis.

Transmission

  • Direct contact through skin abrasions or mucous membranes with infected animals or animal products (farmers, butchers and veterinarians).
  • Consumption of unpasteurized milk products and meat.
  • Inhalation of aerosols from infected animals, or laboratory-associated.

Laboratory diagnosis

Specimen: blood is taken during acute disease. Other samples according to the site of infection, such as joint fluid, CSF, pus. Gram stain is of no significance.

MethodDetails
A · CultureBlood culture is the method of choice early in the disease. Cultures need to be incubated for up to 4–6 weeks as they are slow-growing. It is important to differentiate Brucella from Salmonella by urease test: Brucella produces urease (+ve) while Salmonella does not (−ve).
B · SerologyThe main method of laboratory diagnosis. Two serum samples are collected 14–21 days apart. A wide range of serum dilutions must be done in order to avoid a false-negative result due to the prozone effect, which is commonly present in cases of brucellosis as large amounts of antibodies are produced. Dilutions are done from 1:20 to 1:520. ELISA is used to differentiate between specific IgM and IgG antibodies.
Brucellin testAn intradermal test based on delayed-type hypersensitivity. Interpretation of the result is difficult in persons frequently exposed to infection.
Editorial note

The handout labels the brucellin test “D” although no “C” appears; the dilution range “1:20 to 1:520” is printed identically in both editions and is kept as written — check it against your lab manual.

Prevention

Wearing protective impermeable clothing, rubber boots, gloves and face masks when exposed to animals. Pasteurization of milk. Treatment of domestic animals and live attenuated vaccines for animals (cattle).

Treatment

Humans are treated with combinations of antibiotics for 4 to 6 weeks.

Food-borne poisoning

2.1Staphylococcus aureus

S. aureus are catalase-positive Gram-positive cocci.

The toxin

Staphylococcal enterotoxins do not change the taste, color or odor of food. They are heat stable for 30 minutes of boiling and are not inactivated by brief cooking.

The source

A food handler carrying the organism in the nose, hands, or having superficial lesions such as a boil (abscess).

Common foods

Carbohydrate-rich food, e.g. cake, koshari, and protein-rich food, e.g. milk and its products.

Incubation period is short, 1–6 hours, due to consumption of preformed enterotoxins. The food poisoning is characterized by vomiting and watery non-bloody diarrhea.

Laboratory diagnosis

  • Specimen: food, vomitus.
  • Microscopic examination: Gram-positive cocci in grape-like clusters.
  • Culture: blood agar — complete (beta) hemolysis — and mannitol-salt agar.
  • Typing of S. aureus is done for epidemiologic purposes, in food-poisoning outbreaks. This could be achieved by phage typing and PCR.
Cross-reference · section 2.2 of the handout

Salmonella food poisoning — covered under Salmonella (see section 1.3).

2.3Clostridium botulinum — botulism

Clostridium botulinum is the causative agent of botulism, a rare but fatal form of food poisoning, caused by a potent botulinum toxin.

Transmission and pathogenesis — in the handout’s order
Toxin made in foodproduced within contaminated canned food and ingested preformed
Activatedby proteases in the gastric fluid and by gastric acidity, then absorbed in the intestine
Systemic spreadtransported via the bloodstream
Binds nervesbinds to receptors on peripheral nerves
Blocks releaseinhibits the release of acetylcholine
Flaccid paralysis

Botulinum toxin is rapidly acting; signs and symptoms appear within 12 hours after ingestion. As printed in the handout: “Antitoxin is only effective before the toxin binds the neuromuscular junction within 12 h after ingestion. Prognosis is poor for patients diagnosed after this time period.”

Clinical clarification · antitoxin timing

The handout’s “only effective within 12 hours” is not current guidance and should not be taken as a cut-off.

  • CDC’s 2021 clinical guidelines state that patients with suspected botulism whose symptoms or signs (e.g. paralysis) are progressing should be treated with antitoxin regardless of the time that has elapsed since symptom onset.
  • The greatest benefit goes to those treated within the first 2 days of illness onset — so treat as early as possible, and do not wait for laboratory confirmation once clinical assessment supports botulism.
  • Antitoxin does not reverse paralysis that is already present; it arrests progression by neutralizing toxin still circulating in the blood. Recovery of existing paralysis is slow and depends on supportive care (e.g. ventilation).

For the exam, know what the handout states; in clinical thinking, early antitoxin is better, and later antitoxin can still be indicated while paralysis is progressing.

Sources: CDC, Clinical Guidelines for Diagnosis and Treatment of Botulism, 2021 (MMWR Recomm Rep 70[2]); CDC Clinical Overview of Botulism; WHO Botulism fact sheet.
Clinical clarification · onset, antitoxin product, antibiotics
  • Onset. The handout says symptoms appear within 12 hours. CDC describes foodborne botulism as typically appearing 12–36 hours after toxin ingestion, and in rare cases as early as 6 hours or as late as 10 days.
  • “Trivalent antitoxin (types A, B and E)” is what the handout teaches. The older anti-ABE trivalent product is described in CDC’s guidelines as an older formulation; the product currently available in the United States is the heptavalent antitoxin (types A–G).
  • Antibiotics are not used for foodborne botulism, as the handout says; CDC states antimicrobials provide no benefit in treatment of botulism, and WHO notes antibiotics are not required except in wound botulism.

Diagnosis, treatment and prevention

Laboratory diagnosis

Diagnosis depends on clinical manifestations. The presence of exotoxins in canned food is detected by ELISA.

Treatment

Trivalent antitoxin serum (types A, B and E) is given IV, along with respiratory support. Antibiotics are not used to treat botulism.

Prevention

Swollen cans must be discarded.

Leptospira and leptospirosis

Leptospira are spirochetes that live in stagnant water and lakes.

MorphologySpiral-shaped, motile; one or both ends are hooked. Because they are so thin, live Leptospira are best observed by dark-field microscopy.
CultureAerobic, at 30 °C; need enriched media (Stuart’s media) to enhance growth, which appears after 1–2 weeks.

Leptospirosis (Weil’s disease, 7-day fever) is a bacterial zoonotic disease caused by Leptospira. The infection is commonly transmitted to humans by allowing fresh water contaminated by animal urine to come in contact with breaks in the skin, eyes or mucous membranes. Occupational risk factors include veterinarians, slaughterhouse workers, farmers, mine and sewer workers. The disease is not known to be spread from person to person.

Pathogenesis: incubation period 4–14 days. It is a biphasic disease; the patient is asymptomatic in between phases. The first phase is a variable febrile onset lasting 4–7 days, with hepatitis and jaundice; the second phase is manifested by meningitis (in 40%) with intense headache and stiff neck. Hepatitis and nephritis may recur.

Laboratory diagnosis: organisms are present in blood for the first 7 to 10 days; after this period the microorganism can be found in fresh urine and kidney biopsy. Direct examination: dark-field microscope and PCR. Culture: colonies are identified by agglutination test and DNA probes. Serologic diagnosis: ELISA for IgM and total antibody. There are no human vaccines. Animal vaccines are effective for a few months.

Yellow fever (YF)

A haemorrhagic fever caused by the yellow fever virus, a single-stranded enveloped RNA arbovirus (arthropod-borne virus). The disease is present in equatorial countries in Africa and South America (Amazon basin). Man usually forms an accidental, non-normal host for the virus. The liver and stomach are highly impacted.

Pathogenesis and disease: incubation period 3–5 days. Human activity can bring man into contact with mosquitoes, e.g. clearing of forests, agriculture. The virus multiplies in the reticuloendothelial cells, then viremia occurs and the virus is localized in liver, spleen, kidneys and bone marrow. This is a severe systemic disease in which hemorrhages, and degeneration of the liver, kidney and heart occur. Jaundice is a prominent feature due to liver necrosis. The case-fatality rate can be 50%.

1 · Jungle (sylvatic) yellow fever

The monkey is the main host; the virus is transmitted between monkeys by the Aedes africanus mosquito in Africa. Forest workers or visitors are infected accidentally and carry the infection to urban areas.

2 · Urban yellow fever

The disease is introduced into towns; the cycle is man – mosquito – man. The mosquito is Aedes aegypti.

Yellow feverEarlier-edition text
Laboratory diagnosis1 · Intra-cerebral inoculation of mice with a blood sample. 2 · Direct detection of the RNA virus by PCR. 3 · Detection of a rising antibody titre by neutralizing test, complement fixation, haemagglutination test.
Prevention and control1 · Use of insecticide to kill mosquitoes and their eggs. 2 · Vaccine: “17D strain” live attenuated virus, given as a single dose subcutaneously. “Safe long-lasting protection is obtained for 10 years.” Vaccination is required for travelers to endemic equatorial zones.
Clinical clarification · yellow fever vaccine

The earlier edition says protection lasts 10 years. WHO now states that a single dose of yellow fever vaccine provides lifelong immunity and no booster is needed (immunity develops in 80–100% of people within 10 days). The “10 years” wording reflects older practice.

Source: WHO, Yellow fever fact sheet (who.int).

Dengue, Ebola and Marburg

Dengue and Ebola all affect the liver and the gastrointestinal (GI) system, but with varying severity.

Dengue virus

RNA enveloped virus. The natural hosts are monkeys and man; mosquitoes are the vector. It has an urban and a jungle cycle. Symptoms include fever, headache, rash, bone pain (“breakbone fever”), myalgias and arthralgia. Dengue hemorrhagic fever is a deadly complication of dengue. The earlier chapter gives its case-fatality rate as 50%.

Ebola and Marburg viruses

RNA enveloped viruses that cause fatal disease in man: haemorrhage, fever, diarrhea, vomiting and muscle pain. No known reservoir/vector hosts. They are transmitted by direct contact with infected body fluids.

Clinical clarification · dengue

A 50% case-fatality rate should not be read as the usual outcome of dengue hemorrhagic fever. WHO’s current fact sheet uses the term “severe dengue” and says early detection and access to proper medical care greatly lower fatality rates; WHO-affiliated sources put the case-fatality below 1% with appropriate care. Reported fatality in untreated severe dengue varies by source (roughly 10–20%). Learn the earlier edition’s 50% only as that edition’s statement.

Sources: WHO, Dengue and severe dengue fact sheet; WHO AFRO, Dengue; CDC, Severe Dengue 2015 case definition.
