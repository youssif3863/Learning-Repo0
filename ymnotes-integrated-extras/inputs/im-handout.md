# 01 | GI Symptoms and Investigations | main=1 | subjects=Internal Medicine · Radiology / Investigation

## Symptoms and signs | Main pp. 2–5

::: pearl C1-02 | Why alarm features matter | main=2 | im=1 | section=Alarm symptoms
Many GI consultations are for functional (non-organic) symptoms, yet about **20% of all cancers** arise in the GI tract.
The clinician's main task is therefore to separate organic disease from functional symptoms, and alarm features are the tool for doing so.
:::

::: addition C1-05/06/08 | Vomiting: how it is triggered | main=3 | im=2 | section=Vomiting
- **Vomiting centres** lie in the reticular formation of the medulla. They are stimulated by the **chemoreceptor trigger zone (CTZ)** and by the **vagus**; drugs, motion sickness and metabolic causes can stimulate these zones directly.
- Vomiting is usually preceded by two phases:
  - **Nausea**, with autonomic features (salivation, pallor, sweating).
  - **Retching**, a strong, involuntary, unproductive effort to vomit with contraction of the abdominal muscles.
- Add **vestibular disease** to the CNS causes of vomiting (alongside raised intracranial pressure and migraine).
:::

::: addition C1-09 | Flatulence | main=3 | im=2 | section=GI symptoms
Flatulence means excessive wind: belching, abdominal distension, gurgling, or passing flatus per rectum.
Increased gas production comes from colonic bacterial breakdown of non-absorbed carbohydrate, a high-fibre diet, and carbonated drinks.
:::

::: addition C1-12 | Apparent acute abdomen: porphyria | main=4 | im=4 | section=Abdominal pain
Porphyria is another metabolic cause of an apparent "acute abdomen", alongside diabetic ketoacidosis.
:::

::: pearl C1-13/14/15 | Pain patterns: diagnostic clues | main=4 | im=4 | section=Abdominal pain by site
- **Epigastric pain:** functional dyspepsia is the commonest diagnosis, but its symptoms can be **identical** to those of peptic ulcer disease.
- **Biliary pain** can be felt in the epigastrium as well as the right hypochondrium. It remits spontaneously and recurs weeks or months later.
- **Peptic ulcer** sometimes presents with right hypochondrial pain.
- Chronic, constant hypochondrial pain (right or left) in a **well-looking patient** is frequently functional. Right iliac fossa pain may also be functional.
:::

::: pearl C1-19/20 | Blood per rectum: where is it from? | main=4 | im=5, 37 | section=GI bleeding
- Dark blood and clots per rectum **without shock** always come from a lower GI source. An upper GI bleed produces blood per rectum only when it is massive, and then it is usually accompanied by shock.
- Haematochezia usually comes from a lesion distal to the **ligament of Treitz** (duodenojejunal junction). It can also occur with a massive upper GI bleed because of rapid transit.
:::

## Investigations | Main pp. 5–9

::: addition C1-23/24/25/26 | Endoscopy: practical details | main=6–7 | im=6–7 | section=Endoscopy
- **Sigmoidoscopy** is also used for diarrhoea and change in bowel habit. The **rigid** scope inspects at most 20–25 cm of distal colon; the **flexible** scope can reach the splenic flexure.
- **Colonoscopy:** benign strictures are **dilated**; malignant strictures are **stented**.
- **Balloon enteroscopy** (single or double balloon) can examine the small bowel from the duodenum to the ileum, using specialised enteroscopes in expert centres.
- **Capsule endoscopy:** avoid it if a stricture is suspected.
:::

::: addition C1-29/30/31/34 | Imaging: extra interpretation points | main=7–9 | im=7–8 | section=Imaging
- **Plain films:** chest as well as abdominal films are used in the acute abdomen; interpretation depends on gas shadows inside and outside the bowel. Plain films can also assess **faecal loading** in constipation. CT is more sensitive than plain films for gallstone and chronic pancreatitis calcification.
- **Ultrasound** in the acute abdomen can also show enlarged mesenteric glands.
- **CT:** oral intraluminal contrast (e.g. Gastrografin) with IV contrast improves diagnosis. CT is sensitive to **small volumes of gas** from a perforated viscus and to **contrast leak** from the gut lumen. In **high-grade** obstruction it is usually diagnostic of both the presence and the cause. It can guide biopsy of tumours or lymph nodes.
- **PET** is also used to detect **recurrent** disease.
:::

::: addition C1-32 | CT colonography: how it is done | main=9 | im=8 | section=CT colonography
CT colonography ("virtual colonoscopy") is performed after CO₂ insufflation into a previously cleansed colon. It is an alternative to colonoscopy for diagnosing colonic mass lesions.
:::

::: addition C1-35/36/37 | Contrast studies | main=9 | im=9 | section=Contrast studies
- Gastroscopy replaced the barium meal because it is **more sensitive** and allows **biopsy**.
- **Small bowel follow-through** shows the fold pattern and calibre of the small bowel; dedicated views of the terminal ileum show **early changes of Crohn's disease or TB**.
- **Small bowel enema (enteroclysis):** a tube is passed into the duodenum and a large volume of dilute barium is introduced, to show strictures or adhesions when intermittent obstruction is suspected. It has generally been replaced by **MR enteroclysis**.
:::

# 03 | The Stomach and Duodenum | main=33 | subjects=Internal Medicine · Pharmacology · Surgery

## Anatomy, physiology and gastritis | Main pp. 34–36

::: addition C3-02/03 | Gastric mucus and the functions of acid | main=35 | im=21–22 | section=Anatomy and physiology
- Mucus-secreting cells are present **throughout the stomach** and secrete both mucus and bicarbonate. The mucus is made of glycoproteins called **mucins**.
- Gastric acid is **not essential for digestion**, but it protects against some food-borne infections.
- Other gastric functions: a **reservoir** for food, **emulsification of fat** and mixing of gastric contents.
:::

::: addition C3-05/06 | Classifying gastritis and its causes | main=35–36 | im=23 | section=Gastritis
- Gastritis may be classified by **acuity** (acute vs chronic), by **histology**, or by **etiology**.
- **Gastropathy** (epithelial injury and regeneration with little inflammation) is usually caused by **bile reflux**, **hypovolaemia**, or **chronic congestion** as in portal hypertension.
- Named causes of gastritis:
  - H. pylori gastritis, the **commonest** cause
  - erosive and haemorrhagic gastritis (NSAIDs)
  - autoimmune gastritis
  - viral infection (CMV, herpes simplex)
  - Crohn's disease
:::

::: addition C3-08/09/11 | Acute gastritis: extra points | main=36 | im=24–25 | section=Acute gastritis
- Stress gastritis also occurs in **acute myocardial infarction** and **stroke**, as well as after surgery, trauma, burns and CNS injury.
- Most acute gastritis settles within days to weeks, but some cases become recurrent or long-term, i.e. **chronic gastritis**. Over time this raises the risk of gastric ulcer, upper GI bleeding and some gastric neoplasms.
- Treatment: an **H2 blocker** (e.g. famotidine) may be used along with the PPI. Remove risk factors: stop NSAIDs, reduce caffeine, stop alcohol and smoking.
:::

::: discrepancy C3-13 | Autoimmune gastritis: "pangastritis"? | main=38, 42 | im=26 | section=Chronic autoimmune gastritis
The two sources use the term "pangastritis" differently.
Main book: pp. 38, 42 say autoimmune gastritis affects the fundus and body and **spares the antrum**; "pangastritis" is used only for H. pylori gastritis spreading from antrum to body.
IM: p. 26 describes autoimmune gastritis as affecting the body and fundus and calls it **"pangastritis"**.
:::

::: pearl C3-14 | Where intestinal metaplasia comes from | main=38, 42 | im=26 | section=Chronic gastritis
Gastric metaplasia, usually of the **intestinal type**, occurs almost always in the setting of **atrophic gastritis**. (Your book's Chapter 4, p. 72, states that intestinal-type gastric cancer arises on intestinal metaplasia; this entry supplies the missing first link.)
:::

:::

::: pearl C3-18/19 | Why combination therapy, and who is treated | main=40–41, 48 | im=31 | section=Eradication therapy
- **No single drug** is effective against H. pylori on its own, so regimens combine two antibiotics with powerful acid suppression.
- Resistance to metronidazole and clarithromycin is high in some populations, and oral metronidazole frequently causes side-effects.
- **All** patients with duodenal or gastric ulcer who have H. pylori should receive eradication therapy. Treating incidental infection with **no ulcer** is controversial.

## Erosive gastritis and NSAIDs | Main pp. 36–37, 48

::: pearl C3-21/27 | NSAID damage: how common, and why it can be missed | main=36–37 | im=32, 35 | section=Gastritis and NSAIDs
- Aspirin inhibits the synthesis of both prostaglandin E2 and **F2-alpha**.
- With recurrent NSAID use (e.g. osteoarthritis) erosive gastritis can become **chronic**, and in alcoholics and chronic NSAID users it may be **completely asymptomatic**.
- Only about **5%** of NSAID users have GI symptoms and **1–2%** a major problem such as a GI bleed. It is still a large problem because so many people, especially the elderly, take NSAIDs, including low-dose aspirin.
:::

::: addition C3-23 | Drug options for erosive gastritis | main=48 | im=32 | section=Pharmacology
IM lists three drug options for erosive and haemorrhagic gastritis: **H2 blockers, proton pump inhibitors and sucralfate**. In the main book, sucralfate appears only in the pharmacology table (mainly stress-ulcer prophylaxis).
:::

## Acute upper GI bleeding | Main pp. 50–52

::: addition GB-03 | Drugs and bleeding: two qualifiers | main=50 | im=37 | section=Etiology
- **Even 75 mg/day of aspirin** can produce ulcers, erosions and GI haemorrhage.
- IM states that corticosteroids in the usual therapeutic doses have **no influence on GI haemorrhage** on their own. (Main p. 50 still advises avoiding NSAID–corticosteroid combinations.)
:::

::: addition GB-04/31 | Natural history and prognosis | main=50 | im=37–38, 48 | section=Clinical approach
- Any significant GI bleed within the last **48 hours** should be assessed in hospital.
- About **85%** of patients stop bleeding spontaneously within 48 hours; many need no immediate treatment because the blood loss is small.
- Mortality from GI haemorrhage has stayed at **5–12%** despite advances in management, mainly because patients are now older and have more co-morbidity.
:::

::: addition GB-05/06 | Resuscitation and history: practical triggers | main=50 | im=38 | section=ABCDE; unstable patient
- **Circulation:** unless there are clear signs of a cardiac cause, give IV fluid to any patient with **cool peripheries and a fast heart rate**.
- **Disability:** common causes of unconsciousness are profound hypoxia, hypercapnia, cerebral hypoperfusion, or recently given sedatives/analgesics.
- **History:** co-morbidity; acid-peptic symptoms; alcoholic liver disease, chronic hepatitis or cirrhosis; anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, steroids, alcohol binge; coagulation disorders. Ask about **epistaxis or haemoptysis** to exclude a non-GI source of the blood.
:::

::: addition GB-07/08/09/11 | Transfusion and supportive care | main=50–51 | im=39–40 | section=Unstable patient
- Monitor pulse and BP **half-hourly**; cross-match **2 units** initially. Give plasma expanders or 0.9% saline until blood is available, then restore volume rapidly, preferably with red cell concentrates through large-bore cannulae.
- **Transfuse if:** shock (pallor, cold nose, systolic BP < 100 mmHg, pulse > 100/min), **or** Hb < 10 (IM prints the unit as "mg/dl") with recent or active bleeding.
- Hb falls late because **haemodilution has not yet occurred**. Pulse rate and venous pressure are the best guides to adequate transfusion. Insert a **CVP line** if there is organ failure or a high risk of heart failure, and watch for fluid overload, especially in the elderly.
- A **nasogastric tube** allows gastric wash and monitoring of ongoing bleeding.
- Clopidogrel and warfarin are also stopped. Stopping antiplatelets needs urgent discussion with a **cardiologist**. Manage patients in **high-dependency beds**.
:::

::: pearl GB-12 | Stigmata of recent haemorrhage | main=51 | im=40 | section=Endoscopy
- Endoscopy finds the cause in **80% or more** of cases.
- Stigmata of recent bleeding are a **spurting vessel, active oozing, fresh or organised clot, or black spots**. Their presence means a **higher risk of rebleeding**, which is why they are an indication for endoscopic haemostasis.
:::

::: addition GB-13/14/15/16 | Endoscopic haemostasis: how each method works | main=51 | im=40–42 | section=Endoscopic treatment
- **Why combine:** dual or triple therapy reduces rebleeding more than monotherapy.
- **Adrenaline injection** gives temporary local tamponade and vasoconstriction: 0.5–1.0 mL aliquots of epinephrine 1:10,000 into four quadrants of the ulcer, within 1–2 mm of the bleeding point. Used **alone** it is less effective than other monotherapies. Its main use in active bleeding is **pre-injection**, to slow bleeding and improve the view before definitive therapy. Sclerosants (e.g. ethanolamine) and thrombogenic agents are less efficacious.
- **Thermal:** contact probes compress the vessel and then coagulate it (coaptive coagulation); the **bipolar probe** is the most widely used. **Argon plasma coagulation** is non-contact: monopolar current delivered through ionised argon gas. Other options are the heater probe and laser.
- **Clips** are the therapy of choice for **obvious arterial bleeding**. Unlike cautery they cause no further tissue damage, and they reduce rebleeding.
:::

::: addition GB-19/20/21/22 | Specific causes and after-care | main=50–52 | im=45 | section=Causes; peptic ulcer bleeding
- **Mallory–Weiss tear:** caused by a sudden rise in intra-abdominal pressure. A history of retching **may be absent**. Most bleeds are minor and patients are usually discharged within 24 hours, though the haemorrhage can occasionally be large.
- **After a bleeding ulcer:** continue the PPI for **4 weeks**; always **confirm H. pylori eradication**; if eradication fails, give long-term acid suppression.
- **Bleeding gastric cancer:** bleeds are usually not large, but oozing from the tumour is very difficult to control endoscopically. Surgery is occasionally needed for uncontrolled or repeated bleeding and can usually wait until full evaluation. Radiotherapy is occasionally successful.
- **After PCI:** heparins and clopidogrel increase GI bleeding. Give an IV PPI bolus followed by an infusion; platelet infusion can counter clopidogrel. Stopping antiplatelets carries a high risk.
:::

## Acute lower and chronic GI bleeding | Main pp. 52–53

::: addition GB-25/26/27 | Lower GI bleeding: sorting the source | main=52–53 | im=46–47 | section=Acute lower GI bleeding
- **Unstable patient:** perform **nasogastric lavage**. Blood in the aspirate identifies an upper GI source; a clear aspirate makes a lower GI source more likely. Then proceed to angiography with embolisation, endoscopic intervention or, in extreme cases, surgery. Each of these both locates and treats the bleeding.
- **Stable patient:** abdominal and pelvic CT with oral contrast is used to look for the cause.
- **Angiography** has a low yield, so it is a test of last resort.
:::

::: pearl GB-28/29 | Chronic GI blood loss | main=53 | im=48 | section=Chronic GI bleeding
- The cancers to exclude are particularly **stomach** and **right colon**.
- **Occult blood tests are unhelpful** in this work-up.
- Oesophageal varices usually bleed overtly and **rarely** present as chronic blood loss. **Hookworm** is the most common cause worldwide.
- Do upper endoscopy and colonoscopy **in the same session**. A colonic polyp found at colonoscopy should **not** be assumed to be the cause.
- In frail patients, **unprepared CT** is a reasonable test for colon cancer. Capsule endoscopy yields a diagnosis in **60–85%** but cannot treat.
:::

The site of disease predicts the deficiency:

- **Large intestine:** water, sodium, chloride, potassium, short-chain fatty acids, vitamin K and biotin.

::: addition C5-09/12 | Osmotic and secretory diarrhoea: the mechanism behind the lists | main=79–80 | im=52–53 | section=Classification of diarrhoea
- **Osmotic:** usually a poorly absorbable carbohydrate or a **divalent ion** (e.g. magnesium). The high luminal osmolality draws water into the duodenum and jejunum to make chyme isotonic. Na⁺ then follows the water into the lumen down its concentration gradient, and this Na⁺ pulls in further water.
- **Secretory laxatives** (examples for your book's "laxatives"): ricinoleic acid (castor oil) and dioctyl sodium sulfosuccinate.

::: pearl C5-17 | Why oral rehydration works even in cholera | main=82 | im=56 | section=Treatment of acute diarrhoea

::: addition C5-19 | Clostridium difficile colitis | main=83 | im=57–58 | section=Refractory diarrhoea
Your book names C. difficile only as a cause. IM adds:
- **Pathogenesis:** antibiotics disrupt the normal flora; C. difficile colonises the colon and releases **toxins** that cause mucosal damage and inflammation (originally called pseudomembranous colitis).
- **Diagnosis:** diarrhoea (≥ 3 unformed stools in 24 h) **or** radiographic ileus/toxic megacolon, **plus** a positive stool C. difficile toxin test **or** pseudomembranes at colonoscopy/pathology.
- **Treatment:** stop the offending antibiotic. **Metronidazole** is the drug of choice for mild infection; **vancomycin** for more severe episodes; **fidaxomicin** (a macrocyclic antibiotic) is more active than vancomycin.
- Probiotics are promising for prevention and treatment. **Faecal microbiota transplantation** is reserved for refractory C. difficile colitis. Relapse is common (five or more relapses have been observed).
:::

::: addition C5-21 | Post-infectious IBS: treatable causes to look for | main=84 | im=58 | section=Post-infectious IBS
- Some cases are due to **bacterial overgrowth** and may respond to non-absorbable antibiotics; others are due to **acquired bile acid malabsorption**.
- **Coeliac disease** can first appear after an intestinal infection, so screen for it in this setting.

## Diagnostic tests for malabsorption | Main pp. 93–94

::: addition C5-34/36 | Interpreting the general tests of absorption | main=93–94 | im=68–70 | section=Diagnostic tests
- **Quantitative stool fat:** a borderline 8–14 g/24 h can occur in secretory or osmotic diarrhoea **not** caused by malabsorption. False negative if fat intake is inadequate; false positive with mineral-oil laxatives or rectal suppositories (e.g. cocoa butter).
- **Acid steatocrit:** a cheap, easy screening test for fat malabsorption; normal < 10%.
- **D-xylose test** (used in your book's algorithm, Fig 5.9): tests small-intestinal **mucosal** absorption, so it separates mucosal malabsorption from pancreatic insufficiency. Oral 25 g D-xylose; normal is > 4 g excreted in the urine over 5 h. False positives with renal failure, ascites or incomplete urine collection; it may also be abnormal in bacterial overgrowth and normal in mild or limited mucosal disease.
- **Lactose H₂ breath test:** a **late** peak (3–6 h) of > 20 ppm suggests lactose malabsorption.
- **Faecal elastase-1:** as sensitive as the secretin test for moderate-to-severe pancreatic insufficiency, but unreliable in mild insufficiency; false positives with high stool volume and mucosal disease. The **secretin test** is sensitive but labour-intensive and invasive.
:::

::: addition C5-35 | Diagnosing bacterial overgrowth | main=93 | im=69–70 | section=Tests for bacterial overgrowth
- **Gold standard:** quantitative culture of jejunal aspirate; > 10⁵ CFU/mL suggests overgrowth. False negatives occur with focal diverticula or overgrowth distal to the aspiration site.
- **Glucose H₂ breath test:** an **early** peak (within 2 h) of > 20 ppm suggests overgrowth. Contrast this with the **late** peak of lactose malabsorption. False negatives occur with non-hydrogen-producing organisms.
- **¹⁴C-D-xylose breath test:** sensitivity and specificity > 90%, and more specific than the H₂ breath test. A non-radioactive ¹³C version is suitable for children and pregnant women.
:::

::: addition C5-37 | Biopsy and ileal-function tests | main=93 | im=69 | section=Tests for mucosal disease and ileal function
- **Small-bowel biopsy:** distal duodenal specimens are usually adequate; take several (4–5). Biopsy is **diagnostic** in intestinal infection, Whipple disease, abetalipoproteinaemia, agammaglobulinaemia, lymphangiectasia, lymphoma and amyloidosis. In coeliac disease and tropical sprue the findings are characteristic, but the diagnosis rests on **improvement after treatment**.
- **Permeability studies** are gaining favour for screening for small-intestinal disease and monitoring the response to treatment.
- **SeHCAT:** 7-day retention **< 10%** is abnormal and indicates bile acid malabsorption; liver disease and bacterial overgrowth can give false results.
- The **Schilling test** is no longer used.
:::

# 06 | Constipation | main=95 | subjects=Internal Medicine

## Clinical assessment and testing | Main pp. 96–100

::: pearl C6-03/04 | History and examination clues | main=97–98 | im=71 | section=Clinical assessment
- In **acute-onset** constipation, always ask whether the patient can **pass gas**. Failure to pass gas can signal bowel obstruction.
- **Obstipation** is severe or complete constipation with practically no stool and **no flatus**. It may come with abdominal pain, nausea and vomiting, and points to obstruction.
- **DRE while bearing down:** pelvic floor dyssynergia is suggested when the anal sphincter **fails to relax** or the perineum **does not descend**.
:::

::: addition C6-08 | What anorectal testing measures | main=98, 100 | im=72 | section=Diagnostic evaluation
- **Anorectal manometry:** a pressure-sensitive catheter in the anorectum measures **resting and squeeze pressures** of the anal canal. It assesses the internal and external anal sphincters, **rectal sensation** and expulsion patterns.
- Defecography can also be performed with **MRI** (MRI defecography).
:::

## Disorders of gut–brain interaction and IBS | Main pp. 101–105

::: addition C6-10/11/13/15 | IBS: extra points | main=101–104 | im=73–75 | section=DGBI; IBS
- **Functional chest pain of presumed oesophageal origin** is another oesophageal DGBI: chronic, recurrent pain or discomfort referred to the retrosternum.
- **Autonomic dysfunction** is also listed among the possible contributors to IBS.
- **History:** fibromyalgia is one of the associations found in IBS patients.
- **Limited laboratory tests:** besides faecal calprotectin and coeliac serology, IM lists **CBC** (anaemia or infection), **ESR and CRP** (inflammation) and **TSH** (thyroid dysfunction).
:::

::: pearl C6-18 | Why probiotics are not used in IBS | main=105 | im=75 | section=Management of IBS
Probiotics are not prescribed in IBS because of a **lack of evidence supporting their efficacy**. This is the reason behind the main book's "no routine recommendation".
:::

::: discrepancy C6-16 | IBS diagnostic criteria: which version? | main=102 | im=75 | section=Diagnosis of IBS
The two sources quote different editions of the Rome criteria, so their pain-frequency thresholds differ. The 2-of-3 associated features (related to defecation, change in stool frequency, change in stool form) are the same in both.
Main book: p. 102 (**Rome V**): pain on average **at least 3 days per month** over the last 3 months.
IM: p. 75 (**Rome IV**): recurrent abdominal pain **at least 1 day per week** in the last 3 months.
:::

::: discrepancy C6-17 | Low-FODMAP diet: which foods to limit? | main=104 | im=75 | section=Management of IBS
The example lists of foods to limit do not match.
Main book: p. 104 lists foods high in fructans and oligosaccharides: **onions, garlic, wheat, rye and legumes**.
IM: p. 75 lists **potatoes, brown rice, oats and almonds** as examples of foods the patient should limit.
:::

::: discrepancy C6-20 | IBS-C: the second drug option | main=105 | im=75 | section=Management of IBS-C
Both sources give lubiprostone (chloride channel activator) as the first option but differ on the second.
Main book: p. 105 gives **prucalopride** (selective 5-HT4 agonist, prokinetic). Linaclotide appears only in the laxative table on p. 99, for refractory constipation.
IM: p. 75 gives **linaclotide**, a guanylate cyclase activator that softens stool and stimulates bowel movement.
:::

# 08 | Inflammatory Bowel Disease | main=115 | subjects=Internal Medicine · Pharmacology

## Pathology and diagnosis | Main pp. 116–119

::: addition C8-02/08 | Extra pathological and endoscopic clues | main=117–118 | im=76, 78 | section=UC vs Crohn's (Fig 8.1); Endoscopy
- In IM's comparison table the **rectum is always involved in UC** (only commonly in Crohn's disease).
- On radiology, **"collar-button" ulcers** are common in UC and only occasional in Crohn's disease.
- **Colonoscopy (your book's "Endoscopy" heading has no text):**
  - UC: friable mucosa, extensive ulceration and exudates.
  - Crohn's disease: a **single aphthous ulcer** is the earliest finding; multiple oedematous inflammatory polyps give the **cobblestone** appearance.
:::

::: discrepancy C8-03 | Granulomas in ulcerative colitis | main=117 | im=76–77 | section=UC vs Crohn's (Fig 8.1)
Main book: Fig 8.1 (p. 117) says granulomas **should not be present** in UC (microgranulomas may accompany ruptured crypt abscesses).
IM: The p. 76 pathology table lists granulomas in UC as **"occasional"**. IM's own p. 77 table agrees with the main book.
:::

::: addition C8-11 | More conditions that mimic IBD | main=119 | im=79 | section=Differential diagnosis (Fig 8.3)
- Other colitides: **radiation colitis**, **Behçet's colitis**, **schistosomiasis**, and sexually transmitted colitis (gonococcus, *Chlamydia*, herpes, trauma).
- Bacterial colitis also includes **Shigella** and **invasive E. coli**.
- Crohn's look-alikes: **lymphoma, Yersinia and tuberculosis**.
:::
