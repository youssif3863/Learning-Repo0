## Liver functions and lobular zonation | Book pp. 233–235
::: E17.01 | HEP | Principal liver functions | book=p. 233 | src=Hepatology p. 64 | from=HEP:D-19
Bile formation and secretion; metabolism of carbohydrates, amino acids, lipids and water- and fat-soluble vitamins; inactivation of ammonia (urea cycle), toxins, steroids and other hormones; synthesis of acute-phase proteins, albumin, clotting factors and steroid/hormone-binding proteins; immune function (Kupffer cells).

## Symptoms and signs of chronic liver disease | Book pp. 237–238
::: E18.01 | HEP | Palmar erythema and spider naevi: what they are | book=p. 238 | src=Hepatology p. 1 | from=HEP:A-02
Palmar erythema is persistent redness of the palms; spider naevi are small red skin marks caused by dilated capillaries near the skin surface.
:::
::: E18.02 | HEP | Hyperdynamic circulation and its vasodilators | book=p. 237 | src=Hepatology p. 2 (text + figure) | from=HEP:A-04, HEP:A-05, HEP:A-06, HEP:A-07
- **Signs:** flushed extremities, bounding pulses and capillary pulsations, with increased portal blood flow, low blood pressure and reduced systemic vascular resistance; cardiac output rises.
- **The vasodilators are probably multiple:** formed by the sick hepatocyte, not inactivated by it, or bypassing it through intra- or extra-hepatic portosystemic shunts. They activate the sympathetic and renin–angiotensin systems, promoting sodium and water retention and ascites.
- **Nitric oxide** (endothelium-derived, potent vasodilator) is implicated in the hyperdynamic circulation and is important in ascites, hepatorenal syndrome and portal hypertension. Prostanoids released into the portal vein and endocannabinoids are also shown as splanchnic vasodilators.
:::
::: E18.03 | HEP | Hepatocellular failure: when it occurs | book=pp. 237–238 | src=Hepatology p. 64 | from=HEP:D-18
Hepatocellular failure complicates almost all liver diseases but **not portal venous occlusion alone**; it may be the terminal event in chronic cholestasis such as primary biliary cirrhosis. Its picture and treatment are similar whatever the cause.
:::

## Liver function tests | Book pp. 238, 244–246
::: E18.04 | HEP | What the liver makes, and what albumin and PT tell you | book=pp. 238, 244, 246 | src=Hepatology pp. 1, 5, 10 | from=HEP:A-11, HEP:A-03, HEP:A-12, HEP:A-30, HEP:A-31
- The liver synthesises albumin, **all clotting factors except factor VIII**, carrier proteins (ceruloplasmin, transferrin), cholesterol, bile salts and urea.
- The source links the bleeding tendency of chronic liver disease to failed synthesis of fibrinogen and factors II, V, VII, IX, XI, XII and XIII.
- A falling serum albumin is an excellent indicator of severity and prognosis in chronic liver disease. PT depends on factors I, II, V, VII and X; because these have a shorter half-life than albumin, PT is an **earlier** indicator of severe liver injury.
:::
::: E18.05 | HEP | Liver tests grouped by what they reflect | book=p. 245 | src=Hepatology pp. 6, 87 (table) | from=HEP:F-08, HEP:A-16
- **Excretory / detoxification:** serum and urine bilirubin, serum ammonia.
- **Liver-cell injury:** AST, ALT.
- **Cholestasis:** ALP, GGT, 5′-nucleotidase. 5′-nucleotidase is more specific for cholestasis than ALP but is not widely used.
- **Synthetic function:** albumin, coagulation factors.
:::
::: E18.06 | HEP | ALP and GGT with space-occupying or infiltrative lesions | book=p. 245 | src=Hepatology pp. 6, 20 | from=HEP:A-14, HEP:A-15, HEP:B1-19
- ALP and GGT are markedly raised (&gt;3-fold) not only in cholestasis but also with **space-occupying lesions and hepatic infiltration** (tumours such as cancer, cysts, lymph nodes). Non-hepatic ALP rises occur in bone disease (rickets, osteomalacia).
- In infiltration, bilirubin may be **normal**: partial obstruction lets bilirubin pass yet stimulates ALP/GGT synthesis. AST/ALT are normal or slightly raised (AST &gt; ALT). AFP rises in HCC, and CEA with secondaries from GIT cancer.
:::
::: E18.07 | HEP | Causes of an isolated ALP rise | book=p. 245 | src=Hepatology p. 87 (table) | from=HEP:F-07
Primary or metastatic hepatic tumours, granulomatous liver disease, primary biliary cirrhosis, non-Hodgkin lymphoma, hyperthyroidism, bone disease with rapid turnover, sclerosing cholangitis.
:::

## Bilirubin and bile-acid metabolism | Book p. 245
::: E18.08 | HEP | Where bilirubin comes from | book=p. 245 | src=Hepatology p. 81 | from=HEP:F-01
About 250–300 mg of bilirubin is produced daily; 70–80% comes from haemoglobin of senescent red cells, the rest from prematurely destroyed erythroid cells in the marrow and turnover of haemoproteins such as myoglobin. It is formed in reticuloendothelial cells, mainly in the spleen and liver.
:::
::: E18.09 | HEP | Hepatocyte handling in four steps | book=p. 245 | src=Hepatology pp. 7, 81–82 | from=HEP:F-02, HEP:A-19, HEP:A-17
Uptake, cytosolic binding, conjugation, secretion: albumin-bound bilirubin is taken up (albumin is not), binds **ligandin (Z protein)**, is conjugated to glucuronic acid by UDP-glucuronosyl transferase in the endoplasmic reticulum, and mono- and diglucuronides are actively transported into bile.
:::
::: E18.10 | HEP | In the gut | book=p. 245 | src=Hepatology pp. 7, 82 | from=HEP:F-03, HEP:A-17, HEP:A-18
- Conjugated bilirubin is not absorbed by the intestinal mucosa; only in the distal ileum and colon is it hydrolysed (bacterial β-glucuronidases) to unconjugated bilirubin, then reduced to colourless urobilinogens.
- The primary bile acids, cholic and chenodeoxycholic acid, are made from cholesterol, secreted in bile, and mostly reabsorbed in the ileum for re-secretion (enterohepatic circulation).
:::

## Types of jaundice | Book pp. 245–246
::: E18.11 | HEP | Haemolytic, obstructive and hepatic jaundice: details the book's comparison lacks | book=pp. 245–246 | src=Hepatology pp. 9–10 (table) | from=HEP:A-29, HEP:A-26, HEP:A-28
- Urine **bile salts** (with bilirubin) are absent in haemolytic jaundice and present in obstructive and hepatic jaundice. In hepatic jaundice the stool is **clay-coloured**, as in obstruction.
- In haemolytic jaundice, evidence of haemolysis includes raised reticulocytes **and raised LDH** (the LDH–haptoglobin–reticulocyte algorithm is in Chapter 29). Obstructive jaundice raises serum cholesterol and **β-lipoproteins**.
:::
::: E18.12 | HEP | Crigler–Najjar II, Dubin–Johnson and Rotor | book=pp. 245–246 | src=Hepatology p. 8 | from=HEP:A-21, HEP:A-22
- Crigler–Najjar **type II** = partial UGT deficiency, bilirubin &lt;20 mg/dL (type I: complete absence, &gt;20 mg/dL).
- Dubin–Johnson (defect of bilirubin secretion) has a **darkly pigmented** liver; Rotor syndrome is usually asymptomatic with a non-pigmented liver.
:::

## Viral hepatitis markers | Book pp. 247–250

- An **isolated anti-HBc** in the window period usually means a resolving acute infection that is **not yet completely resolved**, because anti-HBs has not yet appeared.
:::

:::

HAV can be detected by **PCR on faeces**, earlier than serology, but this is very rarely performed.
:::

A positive anti-HCV may be false (cross-reactivity with another infection) and must be confirmed by PCR; a negative result does not exclude HCV (immunodeficiency; very early infection before seroconversion: do PCR). **Anti-HCV IgM is not a marker of acute infection.**
:::

Hepatitis G is spread by contaminated blood or blood products; prevalence in blood donors is about 1%.

## Virology and transmission | Book pp. 254–256
::: E19.03 | HEP | HBV is in every body fluid except stool | book=p. 255 | src=Hepatology p. 16 | from=HEP:B1-06
HBV is present in all body fluids **except stool**, so spread is by blood or body-fluid contact.
:::
::: E19.04 | HEP | HCV and transfusion | book=p. 256 | src=Hepatology p. 18 | from=HEP:B1-11
HCV (enveloped ssRNA flavivirus) is the most common cause of transfusion-associated hepatitis; sexual transmission is low.
:::
::: E19.05 | HEP | Why HDV needs HBV | book=p. 255 | src=Hepatology p. 19 | from=HEP:B1-14
HDV causes infection only when it is **encapsulated with HBsAg**, which is the basis of its dependence on HBV.
:::
::: E19.06 | HEP | HEV genotypes and chronic HEV | book=pp. 254, 258 | src=Hepatology p. 19 | from=HEP:B1-15, HEP:B1-16, HEP:B1-17
Genotypes 1 and 2 cause waterborne outbreaks; genotypes 3 and 4 cause sporadic cases, including via undercooked meat such as pork. HEV affects mainly adults and is rare in children. Chronic (genotype 3) hepatitis E occurs only in the immunocompromised: organ-transplant recipients, cancer chemotherapy, HIV.
:::

## Natural history of HBV | Book pp. 265–267
::: E20.01 | HEP | Who becomes a carrier, and what follows | book=p. 265 | src=Hepatology p. 35 | from=HEP:B2-05, HEP:B2-06, HEP:B2-07
Chronic HBV is more common in males. About 50% of HBsAg carriers have evidence of replication (HBeAg / HBV DNA positive), and about 20% of carriers develop cirrhosis within 5 years of disease onset.
:::
::: E20.02 | HEP | Loss of immune tolerance | book=p. 266 | src=Hepatology p. 35 | from=HEP:B2-08
Immune-tolerant patients (mostly infected perinatally) often lose tolerance in the 3rd–5th decade, when acute hepatitis develops and chronic hepatitis may follow.
:::
::: E20.03 | HEP | Inactive carriers and reactivation | book=p. 267 | src=Hepatology p. 35 | from=HEP:B2-09, HEP:B2-11
Inactive carriers are usually discovered on screening before blood donation. Reactivation can occur with cancer chemotherapy or bone-marrow transplantation, so **antiviral prophylaxis is indicated** in these settings.
:::

## Treatment of HBV | Book pp. 269–271
::: E20.04 | HEP | HBeAg loss | book=p. 269 | src=Hepatology p. 36 | from=HEP:B2-14
When HBeAg disappears, remission is usually sustained for many years.
:::
::: E20.05 | HEP | Pegylated interferon: who responds | book=pp. 270–271 | src=Hepatology p. 36 | from=HEP:B2-15, HEP:B2-16
Pegylated interferon α-2a for 48 weeks gives response rates of about 25–45%; response is best in younger patients with ALT about 3× the upper limit of normal.
:::
::: E20.06 | HEP | Entecavir, tenofovir and lamivudine | book=p. 270 | src=Hepatology p. 37 | from=HEP:B2-17, HEP:B2-18, HEP:B2-19
- **Entecavir:** HBV DNA becomes negative by 48 weeks in 67% (HBeAg-positive) and 90% (HBeAg-negative).
- **Tenofovir** can be used for lamivudine mutations but not alone for adefovir mutations.
- **Lamivudine** (100 mg/day) is well tolerated but limited by resistance from the **YMDD mutant**.
:::

## Hepatitis C (within this chapter) | Book pp. 271–273
::: E20.07 | HEP | How DAAs work | book=p. 272 | src=Hepatology p. 38 | from=HEP:B2-23
DAAs target specific HCV **non-structural (NS) proteins** vital for viral replication, disrupting replication and infection.
:::
::: E20.08 | HEP | Sofosbuvir and ribavirin: dose and adverse effects | book=pp. 272–273 | src=Hepatology p. 38 | from=HEP:B2-24, HEP:B2-26

- **Ribavirin** (adjuvant): 600–1200 mg/day by weight; adverse effects haemolytic anaemia, teratogenicity, sperm abnormalities.

::: E20.09 | HEP | Why Egypt's HCV burden is so high | book=p. 273 | src=Hepatology p. 39 | from=HEP:B2-28

## Varices screening without endoscopy | Book p. 285
::: E22.05 | HEP | Elastography cut-offs | book=p. 285 | src=Hepatology pp. 48, 60 | from=HEP:C-11, HEP:C-12, HEP:D-06
- Liver stiffness **≤20 kPa with platelets &gt;150 × 10⁹/L**: screening endoscopy not needed (large varices &lt;5%).
- Liver stiffness **&gt;25 kPa** is sufficient to diagnose clinically significant portal hypertension (CSPH), with no endoscopy needed; treat with carvedilol to reduce the risk of decompensation.

## Cholestatic and metabolic cirrhosis | Book pp. 287–300
::: E22.06 | HEP | PBC: supplements and biopsy | book=pp. 287–288 | src=Hepatology p. 51 | from=HEP:C-17, HEP:C-18
Supplement fat-soluble vitamins and calcium. Biopsy shows loss of small bile ducts with **ductular proliferation**.
:::
::: E22.07 | HEP | Haemochromatosis: skin, cancer risk and MRI | book=pp. 292–293 | src=Hepatology p. 53 | from=HEP:C-21, HEP:C-23, HEP:C-22
- Bronze skin is due to **melanin** deposition; about 30% may develop HCC as a complication of cirrhosis.
- MRI showing iron in the **pancreas** distinguishes hereditary haemochromatosis from secondary iron overload (haemosiderosis), where only the liver is affected.
:::
::: E22.08 | HEP | Wilson disease: copper handling and features | book=pp. 294–296 | src=Hepatology pp. 12–13 | from=HEP:A-36, HEP:A-37, HEP:A-39, HEP:A-42, HEP:A-43, HEP:A-41
- Ceruloplasmin is an α2-globulin carrying six copper atoms (loaded by ATP7B); 50–75% of intestinal copper is absorbed; normal serum ceruloplasmin is 20–35 mg/dL.
- Haemolysis is due to the toxic effect of copper on red cells; psychiatric disease can extend to psychosis (schizophrenia-like or manic-depressive).
- Kayser–Fleischer rings are gold or greenish-gold rings at the corneoscleral junction; in a few people they are the **first sign** of Wilson disease.
:::

## Primary prophylaxis | Book p. 313
::: E23.03 | HEP | Variceal haemorrhage as a cause of death | book=p. 313 | src=Hepatology p. 60 | from=HEP:D-07
Variceal haemorrhage accounts for about **one-third** of deaths in cirrhosis with portal hypertension.
:::

Non-selective β-blockers reduce portal inflow by lowering cardiac output (β1) and blocking β2 vasodilator receptors on splanchnic arteries; they are **as effective as banding**. Propranolol alternative: 80–160 mg/day, titrated to cut resting pulse by 25%, for life.

- Resuscitate to a systolic BP of about 100 mmHg.
- Terlipressin: 2 mg IV every 6 h, then 1 mg every 4 h, for 2–5 days. The book gives the duration (2–5 days) but no dose; this dose is from the Hepatology source only. (That source's next sentence on ischaemic heart disease is garbled; see note N4.)

::: E23.06 | HEP | Histoacryl and balloon tamponade | book=pp. 315–316 | src=Hepatology p. 62 | from=HEP:D-12, HEP:D-15

- Surgical shunts are usually end-to-side portocaval or Warren (splenorenal).

## Causes and mechanism | Book pp. 338–339
::: E27.01 | HEP | Commonest causes after cirrhosis | book=p. 338 | src=Hepatology p. 66 | from=HEP:E-01
After cirrhosis, malignancy is the next commonest cause; cardiac failure and tuberculous peritonitis are less frequent.
:::
::: E27.02 | HEP | Causes grouped by portal hypertension | book=p. 338 | src=Hepatology p. 66 | from=HEP:E-02
| | Causes |
| With PHT: intrahepatic | Cirrhosis, alcoholic hepatitis, active chronic hepatitis, liver failure, veno-occlusive disease, liver tumour, schistosomiasis, sarcoidosis, Budd–Chiari, focal nodular hyperplasia |
| With PHT: extrahepatic | Heart failure, pericarditis, portal-vein thrombosis |
| No PHT: peritoneal | Carcinomatosis, mesothelioma, pseudomyxoma, infection |
| No PHT: hypoalbuminaemia | Nephrotic syndrome, malnutrition, bowel disease |
| No PHT: mixed | Myxoedema, pancreatic/biliary ascites, amyloidosis, familial Mediterranean fever, granulomatous disease |
:::
::: E27.03 | HEP | RAAS: one activation, two consequences | book=p. 339 | src=Hepatology pp. 67–68 | from=HEP:E-04, HEP:E-05
Renin (juxtaglomerular cells) converts liver-made angiotensinogen to angiotensin I; ACE (mainly pulmonary endothelium) forms angiotensin II, a pressor that also releases aldosterone. Release is driven by renal perfusion (JG cells), distal Na/Cl load (macula densa) and sympathetic β-receptors. The same RAAS activation causes **renal sodium avidity (ascites)** and **renal vasoconstriction (HRS)**.
:::

## Investigations | Book p. 340
::: E27.04 | HEP | Detecting and tapping ascites | book=p. 340 | src=Hepatology p. 70 | from=HEP:E-08, HEP:E-09, HEP:E-10
- Ultrasound detects as little as 30 mL (right lateral decubitus).
- Diagnostic paracentesis is indicated for new-onset ascites or a suspected complication (sterile, 20–23-gauge angiocatheter).
- Extra fluid tests: Gram stain, triglycerides (chylous), bilirubin (bowel perforation), glucose, amylase, LDH, cytology. Pair ascitic with same-day serum albumin for SAAG.
:::
::: E27.05 | HEP | SAAG: accuracy and extra causes | book=p. 340 | src=Hepatology p. 71 (text + table) | from=HEP:E-11, HEP:E-12
SAAG correlates directly with portal pressure; ≥1.1 g/dL (11 g/L) vs &lt;1.1 separates portal-hypertensive ascites with &gt;97% accuracy.
- **Extra high-gradient causes:** fatty liver of pregnancy, massive metastases, mixed ascites, myxoedema, portal-vein thrombosis, sinusoidal obstruction syndrome.
- **Extra low-gradient causes:** bowel obstruction/infarction, postoperative lymphatic leak, serositis in connective-tissue disease.
:::

## Management | Book pp. 341–342
::: E27.06 | HEP | Diuretics: sodium intake, timing and when to stop | book=p. 341 | src=Hepatology p. 72 (box) | from=HEP:E-14
The book already gives bed rest, daily weight and spironolactone 100–200 mg. The source adds a **70–90 mmol sodium diet**; **after 4 days** consider adding furosemide 40 mg daily; and **stop diuretics** if pre-coma (flap), hypokalaemia, azotaemia or alkalosis.
:::
::: E27.07 | HEP | Refractory ascites: two kinds, and what drives it | book=p. 342 | src=Hepatology p. 74 | from=HEP:E-16, HEP:E-17
Refractory ascites (10–20% of patients) is diuretic-**resistant** (no response to maximal doses) or diuretic-**intractable** (complications prevent an effective dose). Look for contributors: worsening liver disease, portal/hepatic-vein thrombosis, GI bleeding, infection/SBP, malnutrition, HCC, cardiac or renal disease, hepatotoxic or nephrotoxic substances.
:::
::: E27.08 | HEP | Large-volume paracentesis, TIPS and shunting | book=p. 342 | src=Hepatology p. 74 | from=HEP:E-18, HEP:E-19, HEP:E-20
Large-volume paracentesis: 4–6 L daily until the abdomen is emptied, repeated every 2–4 weeks; continue diuretics and sodium restriction afterwards. (Albumin replacement, 8 g per litre removed, is on book p. 342 and in the source.) TIPS lowers diuretic needs, renin and aldosterone but may precipitate encephalopathy or liver failure. Peritoneovenous shunting is another option.
:::

## Spontaneous bacterial peritonitis | Book pp. 343–344
::: E27.09 | HEP | Who gets SBP, and which organisms | book=p. 343 | src=Hepatology pp. 75–76 | from=HEP:E-21, HEP:E-22, HEP:E-24
Infection of ascitic fluid may be spontaneous or follow a previous paracentesis. The **spontaneous** type develops in about **8% of cirrhotics with ascites** (the book's 10% is for all cirrhotic patients, a figure the source also gives), especially when the cirrhosis is severely decompensated. 90% are monomicrobial. More than one organism suggests paracentesis-related infection; anaerobes are rare. Variceal bleeding and previous SBP carry particular risk.
:::
::: E27.10 | HEP | From translocation to SBP | book=p. 343 | src=Hepatology p. 76 (figure) | from=HEP:E-23
Gut bacteria translocate (overgrowth, dysmotility) to mesenteric nodes or bypass liver RES via portosystemic shunts → bacteraemia → **bacterascites**, which resolves if ascitic opsonic activity is good and becomes SBP if it is poor.
:::
::: E27.11 | HEP | SBP can be silent | book=p. 343 | src=Hepatology p. 76 | from=HEP:E-24
Classic features (fever, pain, leucocytosis) **may be absent**; the book (p. 343) already says to suspect SBP in any cirrhotic with ascites who deteriorates.
:::
::: E27.12 | HEP | Treatment length and prophylaxis after bleeding | book=pp. 343–344 | src=Hepatology pp. 76–77 | from=HEP:E-25, HEP:E-26
Treat for ≥5 days; amoxicillin–clavulanate is as effective as third-generation cephalosporins. After upper GI haemorrhage (or sclerotherapy) give norfloxacin 400 mg 12-hourly for **at least 7 days**.
:::

## Hepatorenal syndrome | Book pp. 346–347
::: E28.01 | HEP | The kidneys are normal: the defect is blood flow | book=p. 346 | src=Hepatology p. 78 (text + figure) | from=HEP:E-28, HEP:E-29
The book (p. 346) gives the normal renal histology, renal vasoconstriction and splanchnic vasodilatation. The source adds that the kidneys are **capable of normal function** (HRS is progressive, and the defect is renal blood flow), that **cardiac dysfunction** is a third component, and that most patients have tense ascites.
:::
::: E28.02 | HEP | Classic criteria and what to exclude | book=pp. 346–347 | src=Hepatology p. 79 | from=HEP:E-32, HEP:E-33
Classic criteria used serum creatinine ≥1.5 mg/dL (133 µmol/L) persisting 48 h after diuretic withdrawal and albumin expansion. Exclude toxins (paracetamol, NSAIDs, carbon tetrachloride), infection, ATN (hypotension, aminoglycosides, contrast) and obstructive uropathy.
:::
::: E28.03 | HEP | Preventing HRS | book=p. 347 | src=Hepatology p. 80 (table) | from=HEP:E-35
Avoid volume depletion (diuretics, lactulose, GI bleeding, LVP without albumin); use ACEI/ARB/NSAIDs/antibiotics judiciously; treat infection early and give SBP prophylaxis; pentoxifylline in severe alcoholic hepatitis; prevent variceal bleeding.
:::
::: E28.04 | HEP | Albumin loading and alternatives to terlipressin | book=p. 347 | src=Hepatology p. 80 (table) | from=HEP:E-36
- Albumin loading 1 g/kg/day (max 100 g) to a CVP of 10–15 cmH₂O.
- Alternatives to terlipressin: midodrine 2.5–5 mg tds (to 15 mg tds) + octreotide 100 µg SC tds (to 200 µg), or noradrenaline 0.1–0.7 µg/kg/min, titrated to MAP.
- Vasopressors generally for up to 2 weeks; evaluate for transplantation.
The terlipressin escalation schedule and maintenance albumin dose from the same table differ from the book and are kept for review (R36).
:::

## Approach to jaundice | Book pp. 351–354
::: E29.01 | HEP | Isolated indirect hyperbilirubinaemia: the algorithm | book=p. 354 | src=Hepatology p. 10 (algorithm) | from=HEP:A-27
First exclude recent trauma, haematoma or transfusion; then LDH, haptoglobin and reticulocyte count: abnormal → haemolysis/dyserythropoiesis; normal → Gilbert or Crigler–Najjar I/II.
:::
::: E29.02 | HEP | Halothane | book=p. 351 | src=Hepatology p. 9 | from=HEP:A-25
Halothane (like paracetamol) causes toxic hepatitis with mixed (hepatic) hyperbilirubinaemia.
:::
::: E29.03 | HEP | Biliary obstruction vs liver disease | book=pp. 353–354 | src=Hepatology p. 87 (table) | from=HEP:F-06
| | Favours biliary obstruction | Favours liver disease |
| History | Abdominal pain, fever/rigors, prior biliary surgery, older age | Viral prodrome, known viral exposure, blood products or IV drug use, hepatotoxin, family history |
| Examination | Fever, tenderness, palpable mass, surgical scar | Spider telangiectasias, stigmata of portal hypertension, asterixis |
| Laboratory | ALP ↑ out of proportion; PT normal or corrects with vitamin K; leucocytosis; amylase/lipase ↑ | Aminotransferases ↑ out of proportion; PT does not correct with vitamin K; thrombocytopenia; positive serology |
:::
::: E29.04 | HEP | Intrahepatic cholestasis by level | book=p. 352 | src=Hepatology p. 85 (figure) | from=HEP:F-10
- **Hepatocyte:** viral, drug and alcoholic hepatitis; post-necrotic cirrhosis; endotoxin.
- **Canaliculus:** benign recurrent cholestasis; hormones, pregnancy, drugs.
- **Ductules:** PBC, allograft rejection, **graft-versus-host**, biliary atresia, idiopathic adult ductopenia.
- **Larger ducts:** primary/secondary sclerosing cholangitis.
:::

## Pathogenesis | Book pp. 358, 360
::: E30.01 | HEP | Ammonia | book=p. 358 | src=Hepatology p. 90 (text + figure) | from=HEP:F-12, HEP:F-15
About half of gut ammonia is made by bacteria, the rest from dietary protein and glutamine; it is cleared as urea and glutamine. Blood ammonia is raised in **90%** of HE; ammonia damages the blood–brain barrier and astrocytes and shifts neural activity toward inhibition. Reduced muscle metabolism also raises it.
:::
::: E30.02 | HEP | Amino-acid imbalance and false neurotransmitters | book=pp. 358, 360 | src=Hepatology p. 90 | from=HEP:F-13
Aromatic amino acids rise (failed hepatic deamination) while branched-chain amino acids fall (muscle/renal uptake driven by **hyperinsulinaemia**); more aromatic amino acids enter the brain, and phenylalanine inhibits dopa synthesis → false neurotransmitters.
:::
::: E30.03 | HEP | GABA | book=p. 358 | src=Hepatology p. 90 | from=HEP:F-14
GABA is made by gut bacteria and normally cleared by the liver; in liver failure or shunting it reaches the systemic circulation.
:::

## Treatment framework | Book p. 359
::: E30.04 | HEP | Three strategies | book=p. 359 | src=Hepatology p. 91 | from=HEP:F-16
Treat the **precipitating cause**; reduce production and absorption of gut-derived ammonia and toxins; modify neurotransmitter balance.
:::
